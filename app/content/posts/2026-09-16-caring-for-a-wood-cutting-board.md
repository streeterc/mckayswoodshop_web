Wash a wood board by hand with warm water and a bit of soap, then stand it on its edge to dry. Left flat on a wet counter, a board dries unevenly and can start to cup or crack along the grain. Standing it up lets air reach both faces at once — a plate rack or a drying rack works fine. Never put one in a dishwasher; the heat and standing water are exactly what the wood is trying to avoid.

Oil it before it needs it, not after. A board that looks dry, pale, or feels rough is already past due. Food-grade mineral oil or a board-specific wax blend both work — rub a coat on with a clean cloth, let it sit for a few minutes, wipe off what didn't soak in. Once a month for a board in regular use is a reasonable habit; a board that's drying out faster than that is usually sitting somewhere too hot or too dry, like right next to a stove or a heating vent.

Knife marks and light stains are normal and don't mean the board is failing — a light sanding with fine-grit paper, followed by a fresh coat of oil, brings most boards back close to new. If a board ever actually warps or splits, that's the one thing sanding won't fix; at that point it's worth asking whether it's worth repairing or replacing.
