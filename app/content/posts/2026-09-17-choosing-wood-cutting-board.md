Most boards come down to two woods: hard maple and black walnut. Maple is pale, tight-grained, and about as durable as a cutting surface gets — it's what you'll see under most professional knives for a reason. Walnut is darker, a little softer, and shows scratches less because the color hides them. Neither one dulls a blade faster than the other; the hardness difference between them is small enough that knife care matters more than wood choice.

End-grain boards (where you're cutting into the end of the wood fibers, not across the face) are the most forgiving on knife edges and the most resistant to deep scarring, because the fibers close back up after a cut instead of splitting. They're also heavier and pricier to make — more small pieces glued into a checkerboard, rather than a few wide boards edge-glued together. A face-grain board is lighter, cheaper, and still holds up fine for everyday use; end-grain earns its price on boards that'll see daily, heavy use.

Whatever species you pick, avoid anything open-pored like oak or ash for a board that touches food — the grain holds moisture and bacteria in a way tight-grained hardwoods don't. If you're not sure which wood fits how you actually cook, that's a fine thing to ask about before ordering.
